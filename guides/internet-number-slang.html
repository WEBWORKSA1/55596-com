Internet number slang: how the world writes with digits

From Thai 555 to Chinese 520, Korean 1004 and Arabizi 7777 — why people turn numbers into words.

By 55596 Editorial · Updated October 15, 2026

Every language that got online found its own way to write with numbers. Some codes are about speed, some fill in missing letters, and some are wordplay that only works when you say the digits aloud.

Sound-alike codes

Chinese is rich in these because many syllables share sounds. 520 (wǔ èr líng) is close to 我爱你, 'I love you'. 1314 echoes 一生一世, 'a whole lifetime'. 88 sounds like 'bye-bye'. 7456 sounds like 'I'm so angry'. Japanese goroawase does the same: 39 is 'thank you' and 4649 is yoroshiku. Korean has 1004 (cheon-sa, angel) and 8282 (pal-i pal-i, hurry up).

Laughter codes

Thai uses 555 because five is 'hâa'. Chinese uses 233 or 2333, from a laughing emoticon on an early forum. Arabizi writers use 7 for the letter ح, so 7777 sounds like laughter too.

Letter-count codes

English pager slang counted letters: 143 is I (1) love (4) you (3), and 831 is '8 letters, 3 words, 1 meaning'.

Borrowed codes

Some numbers become slang by association: 404 (the web's 'not found' error) means clueless, 411 means information, and 86 means to cancel or remove.

Watch out for collisions

The same digits can mean opposite things: 555 is laughter in Thai and crying in Chinese; 666 is praise in Chinese gaming and ominous in Western tradition. If you're using a number in a campaign, check every reading in the decoder, and browse the full slang dictionary.

Questions

Why do Chinese people use numbers as words?

Many digits sound like words, so number codes are quick, playful and sometimes discreet.

What is Arabizi?

Arabic written with Latin letters and digits, such as 3 for ع and 7 for ح.
