Chinese lucky numbers for business: prices, phones and addresses

How 8, 6, 9 and 168 can lift a brand in Chinese-speaking markets — and why 4 and 250 can sink a price tag.

By 55596 Editorial · Updated October 15, 2026

If you sell to customers who speak Mandarin or Cantonese — in China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore, Malaysia or the diaspora communities of Vancouver, Sydney and London — numbers are part of your branding whether you plan it or not. Here's a practical guide.

The four digits that matter most

  • 8 (八, bā) sounds like 发 (fā), to prosper. It's the most valuable digit in phone numbers, plates and addresses.
  • 6 (六, liù) sounds like 流, smooth flow — 'business goes smoothly'.
  • 9 (九, jiǔ) sounds like 久, long-lasting — ideal for anniversaries and long-term relationships.
  • 4 (四, sì) sounds like 死, death. Avoid it wherever you can.

Prices

Price points like 88, 168, 188, 288, 688 and 888 feel generous and auspicious. Avoid 4 in the last digit and avoid 250, which is slang for a fool. For gift and red-packet amounts, 520 (I love you) and 1314 (a lifetime) are popular for couples; 666 and 888 for business partners. Even amounts are preferred for gifts, with 4 the exception.

Phone numbers

A phone number ending in 8, 68, 88, 168 or 888 is an easy upgrade, and carriers in Chinese-speaking markets auction premium numbers for exactly that reason. Avoid 14 (sounds like 'want to die') and multiple 4s. Use our lucky phone number score to check yours, then see how to choose a lucky business phone number.

Addresses and floors

Many buildings in East Asia skip the 4th, 14th and 24th floors. Estate agents serving Chinese buyers routinely note the address digits, and some homeowners have successfully applied to change a house number to remove a 4 before selling. If you're opening a store, a unit number with 8 or 6 is a small marketing gift.

Dates

Launches, signings and weddings cluster on dates with 6, 8 and 9 — the Beijing Olympics famously opened at 8:08 pm on 8 August 2008. May 20 (520) is an unofficial lovers' day and a big retail moment.

Keep it respectful

Not every Chinese customer cares about lucky numbers, and overdoing it can feel like a gimmick. Use lucky digits where a number has to exist anyway — a price, a phone line, an address — and avoid the clear negatives.
